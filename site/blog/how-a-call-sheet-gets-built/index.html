How a call sheet gets built, and where it breaks on the day

A call sheet is a promise about tomorrow, kept by about forty people who were not in the room when it was written. Here is the order it gets assembled in, and the places it quietly fails.

Nawaf Alahmed 21 October 2026 7 min read

The call sheet is the only document on a production that the whole unit reads. The script is read by a handful of people. The budget by three. The breakdown by heads of department and nobody else. The call sheet is read by the driver, the runner, the gaffer, the client’s marketing manager and the actor’s mother, and every one of them acts on it.

It is also the document with the least time spent on it. It is built last, usually between 19:00 and 22:00 the night before, by one person who has been standing up since 05:00.

A call sheet is downstream of four other documents

Nothing on it is original. Every line is copied, derived or inherited from somewhere else:

  • the schedule, which decides what is being shot and in what order;
  • the breakdown, which decides who and what has to be there for it;
  • cast availability and the deal memos, which decide the earliest and latest you can legally call each actor;
  • locations, which carry the access times, the permit conditions, the gate, the parking and the contact who holds the key.

If any one of those four is stale, the sheet is wrong, and it is wrong in a way nobody can catch by reading it. A call sheet with a location that changed after the schedule was locked looks exactly like a correct one. You cannot proofread a call sheet into correctness.

It is built backwards from the first shot, not forwards from the call

You do not pick a call time. You pick the moment the camera turns over on shot one, and then you walk backwards, department by department, until you run out of night.

Take an exterior with one actor, first shot at 06:30:

  • Camera and grip want 45 minutes from unit call to being ready on a simple setup. Put a dolly track or a crane in the first shot and it is 90.
  • Art needs to dress before anyone lights, so their pre-call is an hour ahead of the unit.
  • Hair and makeup on a straightforward look is 60 to 90 minutes, then wardrobe, then the drive from base camp to set.
  • Breakfast has to be open fifteen minutes before the first person is expected to have eaten it.

Work it through and you get art at 04:00, talent pickup at 04:15, unit call at 05:00, breakfast from 04:45, camera ready at 06:15 so that 06:30 is real and not aspirational.

Eleven times on a call sheet are derived from one number. Move the first shot by thirty minutes and all eleven move.

That is the most common way a sheet ends up internally inconsistent. Somebody moved the first shot at 21:00, hand-edited four of the eleven times, and sent it. The crew arrives correctly, the actor arrives an hour late, and it reads as the actor’s fault.

The top third is the only part most of the crew reads

Assume a phone, one hand, no light, and a reader who will scroll once. That block has to carry the production and the day number, the date and the weekday, the unit call, the location name with a map pin rather than an address, the gate and the parking, the nearest hospital with its own pin and a drive time, sunrise and sunset, the temperature, and the two phone numbers that will actually be answered at 05:00.

The pin matters more here than it does in most places. In large parts of Riyadh and Jeddah a written address gets a driver to the district and no further, and a compound or a warehouse yard can have three gates with one of them locked. A pin and the words north gate, by the generator beat a correct postal address every time.

Sunrise and sunset are not trivia either. Riyadh in late December gives you roughly 06:41 to 17:25, under eleven hours of light. The same city in June gives you about 05:12 to 18:42. That is two and a half hours of difference in what one exterior day can hold, and it decides whether scene 23 gets shot or pushed.

In summer, the heat writes the shape of the day

A Gulf exterior in July does not run 05:00 to 17:00. By 11:00 you are at 45 degrees in open sun, the camera is throwing thermal warnings, and the second AC is managing heat exhaustion instead of the set. So the day splits: roughly 04:30 to 10:30, a long hold, then 16:00 to 20:30 on the back of the light.

A split day puts two calls and a six hour gap on one sheet, and the gap is where the sheet lies. The crew is not resting in a hotel during those six hours. They are in a van unless you say otherwise. Write down where the hold happens, whether it is cooled, and whether food arrives in it, or a third of the unit will quietly go home and come back late.

Prayer times belong on the sheet for the same reason. Dhuhr and Asr fall inside almost every shooting day, Maghrib tends to arrive with the end of your light, and Friday Jumu’ah needs about an hour and a quarter plus the drive. Printing them as scheduled holds costs you nothing. Leaving them off does not make them not happen, it just hands the problem to the 1st AD at noon.

The scene strip has to match the board, character for character

Each row is scene number, interior or exterior, day or night, a one line synopsis, page eighths, cast numbers, set name and story day. A page is eight eighths, so 2/8 is a short scene and 7/8 is most of a page. A drama day in the region tends to land at four to five pages; a commercial day is measured in setups, often ten to fourteen, and the page count stops being the useful unit.

The failure mode is small and expensive. The board says 14 and 14A because an insert was added in the pink revision. The sheet, retyped by hand, says 14. The insert does not get shot, and nobody notices until the edit.

Cast runs on its own clock, and that clock is contractual

Every actor needs a pickup, a makeup time, a wardrobe time, an on set time and an estimated wrap. Behind those sits turnaround, the rest period between an actor’s wrap and their next call, which most agreements put at eleven hours and some at ten or twelve.

If yesterday wrapped at 23:10, nobody can be called before 10:10. A sheet written at 21:00 for a 05:00 call, before anyone knew what time the day would actually end, is already in breach by the time it is sent. Minors are stricter again: a limited working day, a guardian present, tutoring hours inside it, and the only sane way to schedule it is to build the day around the child first and fit the adults around them.

Seven ways it breaks, all of them boring

  1. Version drift. callsheet_FINAL.pdf, then callsheet_FINAL_2.pdf, then a photo of a printout in a group chat. Three documents claim to be the sheet and two of them are wrong.
  2. The late change. The location moves at 23:41. The message reaches the camera team and not the drivers.
  3. One number moved and ten did not. Covered above, and the most common error on any sheet.
  4. Turnaround broken by yesterday. Nobody recalculated after a late wrap.
  5. One language. A unit in Saudi routinely reads Arabic, English, Urdu, Hindi and Tagalog. A sheet in one language is a sheet that part of your crew is guessing at, and they will not tell you they are guessing.
  6. No way to know who read it. You find out at 05:00, in the dark, by counting heads.
  7. Paper outlives the truth. The copy taped up at base camp is the version from before the change, and it is the one people trust because it is the one in front of them.

Six of those seven are not craft problems. They are distribution problems wearing a craft costume.

What a reissue should cost

One tap. A good reissue tells the crew what changed rather than handing them a fresh forty line document, and tells the office who has opened it and who is still carrying yesterday’s.

That is the part of the job Klaket was built around. The sheet is generated from the schedule rather than retyped from it, so moving the first shot moves every derived time at once. It goes out in the language each crew member reads, on a link with no sign-up, and the office can see the read receipts. When the location changes at 23:41, the new sheet is with fourteen people at 23:42 and you know which of them have seen it.

The test is a driver at 04:30

Completeness is the wrong standard. A sheet can carry every field and still fail. The real test is whether a driver who has never worked with you before can open it on a phone, in the dark, in the language they read, and arrive at the correct gate at the correct time without calling anyone. Build the sheet for that reader and the rest of the unit is covered automatically.
